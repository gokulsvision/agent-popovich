# Attacking principles

The rules that sit under every futsal attacking system. Learn these before any named rotation, because a rotation is only a way of obeying them.

**Format:** futsal 5+1. Where an idea survives on an 11v11 pitch, it says so.

## The four lanes

Divide the court into four vertical lanes, roughly 5m each on a 20m-wide court. Keep one player in each lane. That is floor balance.

When two players end up in the same lane, one leaves. That single rule generates most of what looks like complicated movement: a player makes a diagonal run, a teammate recognises it and slides into the vacated position, and the shape is restored.

If the pass to the runner is not on, the runner comes back and the shape resets. A run that fails is not a broken play. Failing to reset is.

Trigger: two bodies in one lane.
Failure: three players crowd the ball side, the far lane is empty, and the switch has nowhere to go.
Cue: **one man per lane. If you are doubled up, leave.**

## Depth before width

Width is easy to remember. Depth is what teams forget.

If all four players stay in their own half, the team plays in a cramped box and every pass is contested. One player holding a high position stretches the defence vertically, which enlarges the space everyone else plays in. That is the pivot's job even when the ball never reaches them.

In a 3-1 the pivot holds depth up to roughly the second penalty mark. Deeper than that and the team is compressed. Any higher and the pass in is unreachable.

Cue: **somebody has to be high, even when the ball is not going there.**

## Pass and move, and the three-second rule

A common elite maxim: never stand still for more than about three seconds when your team has the ball and you do not have it. In a 40-minute match a player has the ball for a few minutes and plays without it for the rest. The game is decided by what happens in those other minutes.

Standing after a pass is an offensive error, not a neutral act. Once you pass, you are unemployed. Find the next job.

Cue: **after you pass, you are unemployed.**

## Occupy the space a teammate left

The cleanest way to find space is not to invent it. It is to fill the space a teammate just vacated. This is the engine of every rotation. Teammate leaves, you arrive, the defence has to make a decision it did not plan.

Cue: **he leaves, you go.**

## Move the defender, then move away from him

Two complementary movements:

- The player **with** the ball moves toward the defender, to commit them and force them to turn.
- The players **without** the ball move away from their markers, to open a passing lane.

Both fail if the passing lane is not clean. Getting closer to a teammate is sometimes right and sometimes exactly wrong. The test is whether a straight line exists between you and the ball.

Cue: **is there a clear line to me. If not, move until there is.**

## Disguise

Elite futsal players are described, approvingly, as liars. Separate where you look from where you pass. Feint before playing. Check away from a marker before going the other way.

Rec translation: this is the last thing to teach. It only works once the basic pass is reliable. A disguised pass from someone who cannot yet hit a firm five-metre pass is just a bad pass.

## Pass weight

Firm, not violent. A soft pass gives a defender time to arrive and time to close the receiver down. A pass so hard it cannot be controlled is a turnover with good intentions.

Cue: **firm enough that nobody else can reach it.**

## Patience against a set defence

When the opponent is organised behind the ball, expect to pass the ball several times before a shot appears. A commonly taught guide is around five passes to disorganise a set defence.

This is backed by match analysis: possessions using more than four passes were associated with greater effectiveness, and possessions of zero to three passes with unsuccessful outcomes ([analysis-metrics](../physical/analysis-metrics.md) (`analysis-metrics`)).

Cue: **against a set defence, move it. The gap opens on the fourth or fifth pass, not the first.**

## Do not lose the ball in your own half

Two specific prohibitions from elite coaching:

- The last man never dribbles. If they lose it, it is a 1v1 against the keeper.
- Avoid square passes across your own defensive area. An interception there is an open goal.

Cue: **last man passes, never dribbles.**

## Finish with a strong shot

A weak shot ends in the keeper's hands and puts you on the back foot immediately. In a game where a turnover becomes a shot within seconds, a tame effort is a defensive problem.

Cue: **hit it properly or do not shoot.**

## Rec translation

This is the whole page for an amateur team:

1. One man per lane.
2. Someone stays high.
3. After you pass, move.
4. Take the space your teammate left.
5. Last man never dribbles.

Five ideas. That is a season, not a session.

## Do not install

Do not teach a named rotation to a group that does not yet do the five things above. Rotations amplify whatever a team already is. A team that stands still after passing will rotate into the same static shape, just more tired.
