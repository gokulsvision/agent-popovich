# Defensive systems

Four families. Most rec teams think they play "man to man" and actually play scramble. Name the family before you coach a clip.

**Format:** futsal positional defence. 11v11 takes the ideas (one trigger, otherwise drop, rest defence), not the court press.

## Man-to-man

Each of the four picks up one opponent. Responsibility is clear. Constant pressure. Simple to explain.

It is not simple to play. It depends on winning your duel. You match their shape, which lets them create space. When one duel is lost, cover is often gone. It is physically expensive.

Principles:

- Opponent, you, then goal. Do not get beaten in behind.
- Distance: at least about an arm's length. Too close and you get spun even if the order is right. The advantage is facing up the court, not raw speed.
- Body so you can see mark and ball. Look at the midpoint of that triangle. If you cannot see both, scan.
- You follow a runner. You do not switch, except on a block or curtain, where switching is the safe answer.
- You cannot chase all game. The trigger to actually win the ball is when the 1st defender has closed to within an arm's length.

At the top level almost nobody plays rigid man-to-man that never switches. Pick-and-roll is how it is beaten. Switching on the screen is still called man-to-man in the broad sense.

Cue: **your man. Goal side. See the ball.**

## Zone

Each player defends an area, or a passing lane. Rare as a pure system in live futsal. Common on set plays. The idea of defending a lane still matters.

Cheaper physically. Easier to counter. Local 2v1s appear. You wait for them to enter your area, so you do not have the initiative.

Two pictures: box-shaped zone (2-2) and Y-shaped zone (see [[press-lines]]).

Cue: **your zone, not their shirt. Do not follow a runner into a pocket you were not given.**

## Mixed

Man-to-man plus zone. Not common in open play. Common on corners as triangle-and-one.

## Alternative (selective / mark-change pressing)

Start from man-to-man references, then build multiple lines once the ball goes wide. Japan have used this. The team changes marks by situation.

This is what good rec defending accidentally wants to be: pick up a man, then compact when the ball is switched.

## Multiple lines

A second line between the presser and the last man. Especially on a high press, the third player narrowing in is the valuable movement. Without it the press is four individuals.

When the structure breaks: slide (two swap), rotate (three or four swap), or retreat.

Cue: **do not all live on one line.**

## Rec translation

One press trigger. Otherwise drop as a block. Nearest man when the ball is in front of us. Jobs on set pieces. That is the whole system. Do not call it man-to-man if nobody knows who they have.

## Do not install

Full-court man-to-man for 40 minutes on a rec roster. A pure zone in open play. A new defensive family the week of a match.
